Entry 4716 SeptemberStudio

A Note on Rain

The east door · 1,610 m

The first rain of autumn arrived two days earlier than the Weather Correspondent had said it would, a fact the Correspondent has asked us to record, and which we have.

No. 031 was on the drying rack by the east door, where it had been for nine days, waiting for its photograph. We heard the rain begin from the long table and nobody moved. There is a rule about this — that a piece already in the weather belongs to the weather — and it has never been tested so directly.

By the time it stopped, the lower half of Low Tide had gone back into the yard. The upper half remained, rimmed and intact, as if it had decided the matter was settled. We photographed it as it was. It is in the catalogue now, under its own number, with its status written in full.

People sometimes ask whether this is sad. It is, a little. It is also the most honest piece we have made this year, and the only one that will never need to be returned.

Rainfall: 11 mm in 40 minutes. Pieces covered: none. Pieces covered on principle: none.

— The Archivist

Entry 4421 AugustObservation

Why We Cure at Altitude

The curing ground · 2,940 m

We are asked, reasonably, why the pieces are not simply left to dry in the yard. The answer is partly practical and partly not.

Practically: at 2,940 metres the air holds less water and gives it up more readily, the sun arrives early and without interruption, and the wind is steady enough to rely on. A piece that would take forty days in the valley takes twenty-three here, and dries evenly, from the outside in, as it should.

Less practically: the shelf is three hours’ walk from anything, and nobody passes it by accident. The pieces are left alone in the way that only distance allows. They are turned once a day, at noon, by whoever has climbed up, and otherwise they are not looked at — which we have found is the most important part of curing, and the hardest to arrange in a studio.

The shelf was chosen because nobody else wanted it. We recommend this as a general principle.

Readings at 12:00 — air 14 °C · RH 18% · wind W 9 km/h · UV 9. Pieces turned: 9. Pieces that appeared to mind: 0.

— The Weather Correspondent

Entry 4130 JulyStudio

Concerning the Thumb

The long table · 1,610 m

Every piece the studio makes is finished with the maker’s thumb. Not as a signature — a signature would be vain, and in any case it is usually on the underside — but because there is a moment, near the end of a sitting, when the rim needs one more pressure and nothing else will do.

We have tried alternatives. A rounded stone is too even. A wooden tool leaves a tool’s mark. Another person’s thumb produces another person’s pie, which the studio has no objection to in principle but cannot catalogue under its own name.

The thumb is not insured. We have enquired.

— The Archivist

Entry 3314 MayMaterial

The Nine Bowls

The library · 1,610 m

The library began with three earths and one sieve. It now holds nine, in open bowls on a long shelf, ordered not by colour but by temperament: the forgiving earths to the left, the difficult ones to the right, and Husk on its own at the end, where it can be watched.

Each bowl is labelled with a code, a name and the place it came from. Each is turned over by hand once a week to keep it from settling into opinions. Nothing is added — no binder, no colour, no water until the day of a sitting — and nothing leaves the library without being written down.

Visitors tend to assume the darkest earths are the most precious. The opposite is closer to true. Pale Ochre comes from a riverbed that floods most years, and some years it simply isn’t there.

Each earth’s entry

— The Keeper of Earths

Entry 319 MayExpedition

Crossing the Red Country

The Red Country · day 4

Day four of the crossing. The hills here are red all the way through, which you only learn by falling over, as one of us did this morning, carefully, onto the bag.

We walk in single file and do not talk much after the second hour. The bags are never set down on the ground — a rule nobody remembers making, observed by everyone. At midday we stop in the only shade for a kilometre in any direction, an overhang that has been used, by the look of it, for a very long time.

Somewhere this afternoon the walking stopped being a way of getting to the studio and became the work itself. It happens every year on almost exactly the same ridge. We have stopped trying to explain it and started looking forward to it.

Distance today: 11.4 km. Water: sufficient. Earth carried: 18.2 kg, unchanged, which is the point.

— The Archivist

Entry 292 MayExpedition

On the Third Chamber

Cave system, name withheld · 968 m

We reached the third chamber a little after eleven. The lamps were dimmed at the entrance, as always, and we went in on our knees.

The floor changes colour here, from grey to a deep rust, over the length of a single step. That step is the edge of Bed 4. It is roughly the size of a dining table, has given us material for every collection since the second, and in all that time has not visibly changed, which we take as permission.

We took eighteen kilograms, a little less than last year. The Maker felt the bed was tired. Nobody argued. We left the usual token — a small, unfired pie, pressed on the spot from the bed’s own earth — and climbed back towards the light.

— The Maker

Nothing of that kind was written down this season. It may still have happened.
